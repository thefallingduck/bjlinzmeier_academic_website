I started out wanting to study dinosaurs. I grew up in rural central
Wisconsin, surrounded by cornfields, and my interest in fossils was
sparked by watching PBS as a child. I made do collecting fieldstones and
enjoyed trips to the coasts of Lake Michigan and Superior to collect
rocks. Later, as an undergrad I worked on mammoth fossils at The Mammoth
Site in Hot Springs, South Dakota. My journey to geochemistry built out
of an interest in statistics and then in animal behavior. Cephalopods
turned out to be the right system for the questions I wanted to ask, and
today I study the chemistry of fossil and modern shells to learn how
ancient cephalopods lived and what the oceans around them were like. [My
2016 paper on *Nautilus*](/publications/linzmeier-oxygen-2016/) shows
where that started and was the result of a project I thought of when I
was a senior undergrad.

If you are thinking about paleontology, I would like to help you see how
work you do now can build toward a career. "What are you going to do
with that?" is a hard question to work through alone, and you should not
have to. I spent time answering the same question when my relatives
asked me before college.

## Can you study paleontology at the University of South Alabama?

Yes. The department offers a [Bachelor of Science in
Geology](https://www.southalabama.edu/colleges/artsandsci/earthsci/geology/majoringeology.html),
and I teach several classes including the paleontology course. We have
small classes with individualized attention, field trips and research
opportunities, and nearby Gulf Coast environments such as Dauphin
Island, the Mobile-Tensaw Delta, and Mobile Bay.

**GY 401, Paleontology (W)**, which I teach and expect to offer next
spring, is a 4-credit, writing-intensive course. We cover evolutionary,
ecological, and environmental processes from the study of marine
invertebrate fossil groups. Its has some prerequisites including EH 102
and introductory geology (GY 111, or GY 112 with GY 112L). See the [full
catalog entry](https://bulletin.southalabama.edu/courses-az/gy/) for the
exact requirements.

This course doesn't focus on remembering the names of hundreds of
species, but focuses on the way paleontologists do science. The key part
is an independent research project: you collect or analyze data in class
and write it up like a research paper. The project is student-led and
intensive, and it pushes you toward independent thinking. We work with
marine invertebrates from across Alabama, and we take field trips in the
state to collect shark teeth and other fossils. Some trips are optional.

Introductory courses are a good place to start. In **Physical Geology
(GY 111)** you learn about Earth's materials and the processes that
shape the planet. In **Earth History (GY 112)**, you trace the history
of the Earth as recorded in its rocks and the life they contain.

## What can you do with paleontology?

A common mistake is to think paleontology skills only lead to
paleontology jobs. They do not. Geology also touches many of today's
environmental questions, and the habits you build while studying fossils
carry over.

-   **Quantitative reasoning.** You learn to work with data and
    statistics.
-   **Independence.** You learn to design a question and see it through.
-   **Learning quickly.** Research means picking up new material, again
    and again.
-   **Geology license.** Complete classes to support becoming a
    [professional geologist](https://www.asbog.org/ "ASBOG") and sign
    off on projects like an engineer.

For graduate school, being able to carry out independent research, or
lead a collaborative project, matters a great deal. That can be easier
to do in a smaller program.

Many students worry about the math, physics and chemistry, and about
jobs. For students planning graduate study, we recommend a full year of
calculus and physics. We also offer substitutions of statistics and
geophysics for those classes.

Not all geology is fieldwork, either. For information on jobs and
salaries, the [American Geosciences Institute's profession
data](https://profession.americangeosciences.org/collections/profession-data/)
is a good independent source.

## Why study fossils on the Gulf Coast?

The range of fossils available near Mobile is large, and Alabama has a
great fossil record that is moderately accessible, especially at
road-cuts, quarries, and along rivers. The end-Cretaceous (K-Pg) impact
is visible in rocks not too far from Mobile. There is also work still to
be done connecting modern oyster fisheries to historical and ancient
ones.

## Doing research with me, even in your first semester

I try to guide and support students toward their own goals. I believe
everyone can succeed, but you have to take a risk and reach out for
opportunities. I am happy to help you get started with research even in
your first semester, and I am open to any kind of project. We will shape
one around what you are curious about and the skills you want to build.

Most projects begin with finding the edge of the scientific conversation
you want to test. I ask students to learn R, QGIS and some chemistry for
many projects, and I am glad to work with you to push past what you
thought you could do. [Rachel Iweka and Alyssa Foster](/abstracts/)
presented soil lead research at the Geological Society of America
meeting. That project was outside paleontology, but it shows how geology
connects to people's lives. Other students have done the Summer
Undergraduate Research Fellowship (SURF) and presented on campus. The
university's [Office of Undergraduate
Research](https://www.southalabama.edu/programs/our/) has details on
SURF and other opportunities.

## Where to start

**If you're in high school:** read about the [B.S. in
Geology](https://www.southalabama.edu/colleges/artsandsci/earthsci/geology/majoringeology.html),
then [apply to the
university](https://www.southalabama.edu/departments/admissions/applytousa.html)
or [request information from the
department](https://www.southalabama.edu/colleges/artsandsci/earthsci/geology/contactus.html).
If you love dinosaurs: the rocks that preserve them are almost as
interesting as the fossils. People study both because the environment
helps explain the biology, and the biology helps explain the geology.

**If you're at a community college:** South Alabama's [Pathway
USA](https://www.southalabama.edu/departments/admissions/pathwayusa)
program works with community colleges including Bishop State Community
College, Coastal Alabama Community College, Lurleen B. Wallace Community
College, and Mississippi Gulf Coast Community College. The registrar's
[Geology transfer
guide](https://southalabama.edu/departments/registrar/areav/geology.html)
lists the courses from Alabama community colleges that count toward a
geology degree.

**If you're already at South Alabama and thinking about a geology
major:** if the fossil record or Earth's history in Physical Geology or
Earth History has caught your attention, send me a message or talk to
the department. The [Geology
page](https://www.southalabama.edu/colleges/artsandsci/earthsci/geology/majoringeology.html)
links the change-of-major forms, and the [geology
minor](https://www.southalabama.edu/colleges/artsandsci/earthsci/geology/minoringeology.html)
is another option.
